How the scoring works

Methodology & Precedent

This platform's rubric is built from published technology transfer frameworks and how peer institutions structure the same decision, not a made up scoring model.

The core frameworks behind each score

TRL Technology Readiness Level
A nine point scale, originally developed at NASA in the 1970s, describing how far a technology has progressed from basic principle toward a demonstrated, market ready product. Used broadly across NSF's I Corps program and university tech transfer offices to gauge technical maturity before a licensing or venture decision is made.
ARL Adoption Readiness Level
A newer companion framework, piloted through U.S. Department of Energy programs, built specifically because TRL does not capture commercialization risk such as product market fit, demand pull, supply chain readiness, workforce, or permitting. This platform's adoption risk factors are modeled on that gap.
AUTM The three decision framework
Technology transfer offices generally evaluate early stage inventions to make three decisions: whether to file a patent, whether to actively market it for licensing, and whether it is better suited to a startup or venture path than an established licensee. This platform mirrors that same fork.
Demand first, not technology first
The scoring here is deliberately ordered so market demand is checked before a path is chosen, not after. A patent only becomes a license or venture candidate if the evaluator finds real evidence of demand in the disclosure itself, not just technical merit or an inventor's own assertion that a market exists. If demand looks weak or unsupported, the patent is flagged as insufficient demand regardless of how sophisticated the underlying technology is. Only once demand clears that bar does the model decide whether the opportunity is narrow enough to fit an existing corporate's roadmap, which points toward licensing, or broad enough that no incumbent already serves it well, which points toward a new venture. Every evaluation also has to name the single strongest reason it could be wrong, a deliberate check against the model simply agreeing with the disclosure's own framing of itself.
Inventor input, funding source, and a marketing abstract
Each case file has an optional staff and inventor input panel, known industry contacts, the inventor's own read on feasibility, willingness to engage, and funding source. When filled in, this is weighed as real evidence, stronger than the disclosure's own unsupported claims, but the evaluation still notes it is self reported, not independently verified. A federal funding source triggers a compliance note about the government's retained use license and possible march-in rights under the Bayh-Dole Act. Every evaluation also generates a separate marketing abstract, plain language, non confidential, written for a business audience rather than a patent examiner, modeled on how offices like Stanford's OTL market technology to industry with inventor-informed, non-technical descriptions rather than the legal abstract itself.
The value score is computed, not guessed
Every patent gets a letter tier (A through D) and a points total out of 15. Thirteen points come from the evaluation itself: market demand verdict, technology readiness level, adoption risk, whether a real licensee or buyer was named with a source rather than just a sector guess, and whether staff or the inventor confirmed real engagement. Up to two more points come from South Carolina investment alignment, described below. The letter thresholds stay at 10, 7, and 4, so alignment can raise a grade and cannot raise the bar for A. It is computed with a fixed formula, not asked of the AI, so the same inputs always produce the same score and every point is traceable back to something already on the page. It deliberately does not produce a licensing-value dollar figure. The dollar amount in the alignment note is PitchBook's reported median check size, not a sum of capital and not a valuation of the patent. Backward citation counts and patent family IDs are shown for reference on live registry pulls but are not folded into this score. Evaluations stored before this change keep the score they were given. Re-run one to include the alignment points.
South Carolina investment alignment
A separate reference table counts completed South Carolina deals from 2023 through 2026 year to date, by PitchBook industry group, with the focus-area labels already on that export. Software and IT, medical devices, life sciences, advanced materials, agriculture, and energy and grid are the stated focus areas. Each patent is mapped to one industry group from its CPC codes and from keywords in the title and abstract. When that local read is uncertain, the ranking model or the full evaluation may pick a group from the same closed list. The score is 0 to 100: deal volume on a log scale, the share of those deals in 2025 or 2026, and 40 points when the group is a stated focus. Seventy or higher adds two value-score points. Fifty-five or higher adds one. In the quick rank, 75 or higher can lift Low or Needs work to Medium, and 80 or higher can lift Medium to High, but only when an abstract is present and the estimated term has not run out. A High tier is never lowered because the match is weak. Investor names are not sent to the model. The registry can filter to a focus area, a score band, or an unmatched technology.
Evaluation runs as two calls, not one
Every patent first gets a fast, search free core evaluation, TRL, demand, path, risk, marketing abstract, all in one call with no web search involved. Unless that read is already a no-demand hard pass, a second call searches for who to sell or license the patent to and stores that list on the evaluation. The list aims for as many real organizations as the search supports, and a marketable patent needs at least three. Fewer than three is a hard pass, not a padded list. This split exists because a combined call, doing full evaluation and web research together, could run long enough on a niche technical topic to exceed Netlify's fixed 60 second execution limit, which cannot be raised through any setting or plan upgrade. If the licensing search fails, the already successful core evaluation is kept rather than the whole result being thrown away. Results stored before this list existed say so, and Re-evaluate is what adds it.
Portfolio ordering and archiving
The Portfolio table sorts by value score, highest tier first, with anything not yet evaluated pushed to the bottom rather than mixed in by recency. Archiving a patent, from its case file or directly from the Portfolio table, removes it from that main view and from the dashboard's active counts without deleting anything, the full evaluation stays exactly as it was. An archived patent remains reachable through "Show archived" on the Portfolio tab, and can be restored to active at any time, which simply reverses the same flag, nothing about its stored evaluation changes either way.
Patentability & landscape screen is a pre-attorney triage, not a legal opinion
On demand, from any case file, this runs a public web search for similar existing patents, competing commercial products, and anything that looks like a potential blocking concern, then suggests a handful of draft patent claim concepts as discussion starting points, never drafted legal language. It is explicitly not a Freedom to Operate search or a comprehensive prior art search, an AI web search cannot access full patent databases and carries none of a licensed attorney's legal judgment or liability. Its purpose is narrower and more honest: help decide whether a technology is worth the cost of sending to the attorney at all. Staff can leave feedback on a run and re-run it, which the next search takes into account and states plainly in its rationale.
Outreach drafts are written from already-verified facts, not new claims
Each named licensing destination can get a "Draft outreach email" button. That drafting step uses no web search of its own. It writes only from the organization name, the role, and why it fits, which the licensing search already returned, so it cannot introduce a new, unverified claim about the recipient. Every draft is a starting point for CURF staff to review, personalize, and send themselves. This app never sends anything on its own.
Publication Watch is public search only, stated plainly
A weekly automatic scan and a manually triggerable one search for new Clemson research publications, using titles, abstracts, press releases, and anything else already publicly indexed, then give each a quick commercial relevance read. It cannot get behind Clemson library's paywalled journal subscriptions, that would need a specific integration with Clemson's own library systems that is not built here, this is a real, stated gap, not an oversight. A finding is only stored and only triggers an email alert once, the first time it appears, re-finding the same publication on a later scan does not repeat the alert. The manual button runs as a genuine Netlify Background Function with a 15 minute execution budget; the automatic weekly run is a scheduled function instead, which Netlify caps at 30 seconds, half of the ordinary 60 second limit the rest of this app is tuned around, so it deliberately runs a single, narrower search rather than risk failing silently every week.

What peer institutions have built

CURF itself, today
Clemson University Research Foundation already runs a searchable catalog of technologies available for licensing and pairs graduate interns with Technology Commercialization Officers to do early market opportunity evaluations by hand. This platform is meant to sit upstream of that work as a faster first pass across the whole portfolio, not to replace the judgment CURF staff already apply.
Flintbox, originally built at the University of British Columbia
A public marketplace where universities post available IP for outside companies to browse, later acquired by Wellspring and now used by hundreds of institutions, with tens of thousands of licenses executed through it. This is the outward facing catalog half of the problem; this platform focuses on the inward facing triage step that should happen before something is worth listing there.
NSF I Corps, including Clemson's own Southeast Hub
A federally funded program, which Clemson recently joined as a regional hub, that trains researchers to test commercial viability directly with customers before committing to a startup path. The venture recommendation track in this platform is meant to feed candidates toward exactly that kind of program rather than send every patent straight to a cold licensing pitch.
I do not have a verified, current source for the exact licensing revenue or deal counts these platforms report today, since figures like that change year to year. Treat any number in this section as approximate and confirm against CURF's own reporting or the vendor's current materials before citing it externally.

Where the Clemson Registry data comes from, and its limits

Google Patents Public Data on BigQuery, not a Google Patents scraper
The live Clemson Registry pull queries Google's patents-public-data.patents.publications table for US records whose assignee name contains Clemson, most recent first. The default list on this tab is a manually checked snapshot and does not need that query. Google Patents has no public bulk query API suited to this, and scraping it at volume sits outside Google's terms of service, so the published BigQuery dataset is the path this app actually uses.
What it cannot do
This registry only surfaces patents Clemson or CURF actually holds. It cannot identify patents by alumni, faculty, or staff assigned to other companies, since patent records carry no education or employment history field. That kind of association has to be verified by a person who already knows it, which is why the app supports tagging a patent as staff verified Clemson affiliated rather than guessing at it automatically.
Sector tags are a first pass, not a classification
Each patent's sector tag is generated from keywords in its title and abstract, loosely grouped along the same five sector split WIPO uses in its own technology concordance work (Electrical Engineering, Instruments, Chemistry, Mechanical Engineering, and an Other Fields catch all). It is not a lookup against official CPC or IPC classification codes, and should be confirmed, not assumed, before it drives a real licensing or venture decision.
