How the scoring works

How we score

A case moves through six steps. The scores below follow published technology-transfer guides and the same choices other universities make. They are not a made-up grade.

How a case moves

1 Scan
On Triage, click Scan for new patents. The scan looks in Google's public patent records and at the USPTO for US patents whose owner name contains Clemson. The same document from both places is one row. A patent application and the patent it later becomes stay separate. Before a scan is saved, the page shows the checked Aug 29, 2026 list. On Publications, click Scan for new papers. That scan reads the Clemson news feed, PubMed, OpenAlex, and arXiv. Each paper gets a quick score: High, Moderate, Low, or Not scored.
2 Quick rank
Click Rank patents with no rank yet. The rank is a first look from the title and summary: High, Medium, Low, Needs work, or Not ranked yet. It is not the full review. South Carolina fit is a separate score from 0 to 100. It matches the patent to one South Carolina industry and counts deals since 2023, how recent those deals are, and whether the industry is a CURF focus area. 70 or higher can add two points to the later patent score. 55 or higher can add one. A strong fit can lift a lower rank. It never lowers a High rank. The dollar in the note is a typical deal size, not the value of this patent.
3 Full review
Pick rows and click Evaluate selected. A patent you already reviewed opens right away. The review reads the patent, then looks for organizations that could buy or license it. The case needs at least three named organizations. Fewer than three is marked No real market. The list is not filled with guesses. When there is a real market, the review also looks for pilot sites. A site with a public page about earlier work in the same area is listed first. A site with no page shows No proof link and is listed last. The patent score is out of 15. The page says what raised it, what lowered it, and the fastest way to raise it, using facts from this patent. The score is not a dollar value.
4 Portfolio
Finished reviews stay on Portfolio. Patents and publications are separate lists. A row added in the last 7 days shows New until you open it, or until you click Mark all seen. If any new rows are waiting, the list opens on New. Opening a case does not run the review again. Re-evaluate is what replaces a review. On a patent, Add pilot sites or Refresh pilot sites updates only that section.
5 Papers
A paper uses a Venture Readiness score from 0 to 100, not the patent score. 75 to 100 is ready to pursue. 55 to 74 is promising, but lower the risk first. 35 to 54 is early research. Below 35 is not ready to sell. The next step can be file with CURF, look at a startup, ask for early funding, find a company partner, license the know-how, or check back later. The filing clock is one year from the publication date. It is red when that year has passed, amber while the US window is still open, green before publication, and unknown when there is no exact date. A paper does not fail just because fewer than three buyers are named. This is not legal advice. Check with CURF or a patent lawyer.
6 Tools
Tools can look up one patent by number or by words in the title and summary. That search uses the list already on Triage. You can also describe a problem. The app checks Clemson patents, then the wider field. If a company wrote the problem, do not paste it unless they said you may share it this way.

Where each score comes from

TRL Technology Readiness Level
A 1 to 9 scale from NASA. 1 is an idea. 9 is a product that works in the real world. Universities use it to see how far along a technology is before they license it or start a company.
ARL Adoption Readiness Level
A companion to the 1 to 9 scale. It covers risks the scale misses, such as whether anyone will buy it, whether the supply chain is ready, and whether people and permits are in place. The adoption risks on a case follow that idea.
AUTM The three decision framework
University offices usually make three calls: file a patent or not, try to license it or not, and start a company or license to a company that already exists. This app follows that same fork.
Demand first, not technology first
We look for a real buyer before we pick a path. A patent becomes a license or startup candidate only when the text itself shows demand. The inventor saying a market exists is not enough. If demand is weak, the case is marked weak market, even if the science is strong. After that, a narrow fit with one company points to a license. A wide gap no company already fills points to a startup. Every review also names the strongest reason it could be wrong.
Inventor input, funding source, and a marketing abstract
Each case can include staff notes: company contacts, whether the inventor will help, how they see the market, and who paid for the work. Those notes count as evidence, and the review still says they come from the inventor, not from an outside check. Federal funding adds a note that the government keeps some rights under the Bayh-Dole Act. Every review also writes a short public summary for a business reader, not for a patent examiner.
Papers use a separate company-readiness score
A paper is not graded like a patent. It does not fail just because fewer than three buyers are named. The AI only pulls facts from the abstract. The app then adds the points. The score is 0 to 100. What is new is 20. How far along it is, is 15. Whether it can be protected is 20. Who needs it is 20. The path to a product is 10. Signs of a founding team are 10. Fit with South Carolina is up to 5. 75 to 100 is ready to pursue. 55 to 74 is promising, but lower the risk first. 35 to 54 is early research. Below 35 is not ready to sell. The next step can be file with CURF, look at a startup (NSF I-Corps, then SCRA or SC Launch), ask for early funding, find a company partner, license the know-how, or check back later. The filing clock is red when the paper is more than a year old, amber during the one-year US window, and green before publication. If there is no exact date, the clock stays unknown. Buyer names and pilot sites are still looked up. A paper does not need them to stay in review. This is not legal advice. Check with CURF or a patent lawyer. Patents keep their own score.
The value score is computed, not guessed
Every patent gets a letter, A through D, and a score out of 15. Thirteen points come from the review: market interest, how far along the technology is, adoption risk, whether a real buyer is named with a link, and whether the inventor or a company contact is confirmed. Up to two more points come from South Carolina fit, below. The letter lines stay at 10, 7, and 4, so fit can raise a grade and cannot raise the line for A. The math is fixed. The AI does not pick the number. The same inputs always give the same score, and every point is on the page. The score is not a dollar value. The dollar in the South Carolina note is a typical deal size, not the value of this patent. Citation counts are shown for reference and are not in the score. Older reviews keep the score they were given. Run the review again to add the fit points.
South Carolina investment alignment
A table counts finished South Carolina deals from 2023 through 2026, by industry. Focus areas are software and IT, medical devices, life sciences, advanced materials, agriculture, and energy and the grid. Each patent is matched to one industry from its patent classes and from words in the title and summary. If that match is unsure, the rank or the full review may pick a group from the same list. The fit score is 0 to 100: how many deals, how many are recent, and 40 points if the group is a focus area. 70 or higher adds two score points. 55 or higher adds one. In the quick rank, 75 or higher can lift Low or Needs work to Medium, and 80 or higher can lift Medium to High, but only when a summary is present and the patent term has not run out. A High grade is never lowered because the match is weak. Investor names are not sent to the AI. You can filter the list by focus area, score, or no match.
A patent review is three steps
First the app reads the patent with no web search: how far along it is, whether anyone wants it, the path, the risks, and a short public summary. Unless that read already finds no market, a second step searches for who might buy or license it and saves that list. A patent with a real market needs at least three named organizations. Fewer than three is marked No real market. The list is not padded. When that list has three or more names, a third step searches for pilot sites. Each site needs a public page about earlier work in the same area. A site with no page shows No proof link and is listed last. If the case is No real market, pilot sites are marked not a good next step, and no pilot search is run. The steps are split because one long search can hit the 60 second server limit. If the buyer search fails, the first read is kept. Older reviews that have no buyer list say so. Re-evaluate is what adds the list. Add pilot sites or Refresh pilot sites updates only the pilot section.
Portfolio ordering and archiving
The patent list sorts by score, highest first. Patents that are not reviewed yet sit at the bottom. Archive hides a case from the main list and from the counts. It does not delete the review. Choose Archived or All to see it again. Restore puts it back. The review itself does not change.
The patent screen is a first look, not legal advice
From a case, you can search the public web for similar patents, products already for sale, and anything that might block use. It also suggests a few claim ideas to discuss. Those are not legal claims you can file. This is not a freedom-to-operate opinion and not a full prior-art search. A web search cannot see every patent database, and it is not a lawyer. Use it to decide whether the technology is worth sending to a lawyer. You can leave a note and run it again. The next search says how that note changed the result.
Outreach drafts are written from already-verified facts, not new claims
Each named buyer can get a Draft outreach email button. That step does not search the web. It writes only from the name, the role, and why it fits, which the buyer search already found. It cannot add a new claim about the company. Every draft is a start for CURF staff to edit and send. This app never sends email.
The paper scan uses public sources only
A weekly scan, and a button you can click, look for new Clemson papers in public titles, summaries, and news. Each one gets a quick "is this commercial?" read. The scan cannot open paid journal articles. That would need the Clemson library, and that link is not built. A paper is saved once and emailed once. Finding it again does not send another email. The button can run for up to 15 minutes. The weekly scan has 30 seconds, so it runs a smaller search on purpose.

What peer institutions have built

CURF itself, today
CURF already lists technologies for license and has graduate interns work with technology managers on early market reviews by hand. This app is a faster first sort before that work. It does not replace the judgment CURF staff already use.
Flintbox, originally built at the University of British Columbia
A public marketplace where universities post inventions companies can browse. Wellspring later bought it. Hundreds of schools use it. This app does the earlier step: decide what is worth listing there.
NSF I Corps, including Clemson's own Southeast Hub
A federal program Clemson joined as a regional hub. It trains researchers to test an idea with customers before they start a company. A startup recommendation here is meant to point people toward that kind of program, not to send every patent into a cold license pitch.
I do not have a checked, current source for the license revenue or deal counts these tools report today. Those numbers change every year. Treat any number here as a rough figure. Confirm it with CURF or the vendor before you quote it outside.

Where the Clemson patent list comes from, and what it cannot do

Google's public patent table, not a scrape of the website
A live scan asks Google's public patent table for US patents whose owner name contains Clemson, newest first. It also asks the USPTO when that key is set. If the USPTO key is missing, the Google list still saves. The same document from both places is one row. A patent application and the patent it later becomes stay separate. The list you see before a scan is the checked Aug 29, 2026 list and does not need that query. Google Patents has no public bulk search that fits this job, and copying the website at volume breaks Google's terms. The published data table is what this app uses.
What it cannot do
This list only shows patents Clemson or CURF owns. It cannot find patents owned by another company, even if a Clemson graduate or employee is an inventor. Patent records do not list school or job history. A person who already knows that link has to confirm it. The app can mark a patent as staff-checked. It does not guess.
Sector tags are a first pass, not a classification
Each sector tag comes from words in the title and summary. The groups follow the five buckets WIPO uses: electrical engineering, instruments, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and other. It is not an official patent-class lookup. Check the tag before you use it to license or start a company.
